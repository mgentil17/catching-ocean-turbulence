# Why vertical turbulent mixing matters

## Connecting the surface ocean and the interior

The ocean is **stratified**: light water lies above dense water. In the tropics, a warm surface mixed layer of a few tens of metres sits on top of a thin layer where temperature and density change quickly with depth, the **thermocline**, and below it the colder ocean interior.

The surface layer is where the ocean exchanges heat, freshwater and gases with the atmosphere, and where there is enough light for photosynthesis. The interior stores cold water, nutrients and carbon, and is often poorer in oxygen. Many properties therefore have strong vertical gradients across the thermocline ({numref}`fig-vertical-exchange`).

:::{figure} figures/vertical_exchange.png
:label: fig-vertical-exchange

:::

Stratification resists vertical motion: lifting dense water costs energy. One of the main ways properties cross it is small-scale **turbulent mixing**, which acts down the gradients. Mixing happens at all depths and has many sources: winds, breaking internal waves, tides flowing over topography, shear between currents ({numref}`fig-cimoli-1`).

:::{figure} figures/cimoli2026_fig1.png
:label: fig-cimoli-1
Turbulent mixing is found at all depths and has many origins. Background colours show the global meridional overturning circulation. Reproduced from Cimoli et al. (2026, Fig. 1), *Nature Communications*, licensed under CC BY-NC-ND 4.0.
:::

A classic example shows why these small scales matter at large scale. Dense water sinks at high latitudes and fills the deep ocean. To keep the deep ocean in a steady state, this water must slowly come back up, which requires mixing it with the lighter water above. Munk (1966) estimated that this needs an average vertical diffusivity of about $10^{-4}$ m² s⁻¹. Yet a tracer released in the thermocline of the open ocean spread vertically with a diffusivity ten times smaller, about $10^{-5}$ m² s⁻¹ (Ledwell et al., 1993). Part of the answer is that mixing is very uneven: it is much stronger in some places, for example over rough topography, than in others (Polzin et al., 1997). **Where and when the ocean mixes matters as much as how much it mixes.**

## Climatic and biogeochemical reach

Vertical mixing does not only matter on timescales of centuries. It also shapes ocean properties from days to seasons and years ({numref}`fig-cimoli-3`). A few examples from Cimoli et al. (2026):

- **Sea surface temperature and rainfall.** In the Indonesian Throughflow, tidal mixing cools surface waters, which affects air–sea heat fluxes and precipitation.
- **Nutrients and life.** In subtropical gyres, mixing supplies part of the nutrients that sustain primary productivity.
- **Climate variability.** Mixing influences the development and persistence of El Niño and La Niña.
- **Carbon and oxygen.** Mixing modulates air–sea carbon fluxes, and carries ventilation tracers from the surface into the interior.

Most of these processes are poorly represented in climate models. Turbulence occurs at scales far smaller than a model grid cell, so its effect has to be **parameterized**, often with simple or even constant diffusivities in the ocean interior (Cimoli et al., 2026). Improving these parameterizations needs observations of mixing, and these are still scarce.

To measure turbulence, we first need to know what it is, and which quantity to measure. This is the topic of the next part.

:::{tip} Take-home
- The ocean is stratified, and vertical turbulent mixing is one of the main ways heat, salt, oxygen, nutrients and carbon cross the stratification.
- Its effects are seen from seasonal sea surface temperature to the global overturning circulation.
- Mixing is very uneven in space and time, and is poorly represented in models: we need observations.
:::
