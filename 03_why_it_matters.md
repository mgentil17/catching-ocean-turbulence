# 3. Why does it matter?

Part 2 showed that turbulence mixes heat, nutrients and oxygen across the thermocline. Why does it matter? We now look at what this mixing does at a larger scale, over a whole season and a whole region, for each of the three properties of our profile ({numref}`fig-profile`).

% Time: ~20 min. One idea and one figure per section. No calculation here: the fluxes are computed in part 5.

## 3.1 Heat: cooling the surface

The clearest example comes from the equatorial Pacific, where a mooring measured turbulence for a whole year ({numref}`fig-moum`). The sun and the atmosphere heat the surface layer (red), while turbulence carries heat down below it (blue). From February to May, heating wins and the surface warms. From June to September, turbulent cooling wins and the surface cools (Moum et al., 2013). The same holds in the Atlantic cold tongue, where turbulent mixing is the main cooling term during the cold season (Hummels et al., 2013).

:::{figure} figures/moum2013_fig3a.png
:label: fig-moum
:width: 70%
One year at the equator, 140°W (Pacific), from January to January. Black: sea surface temperature. Red: heat brought into the surface layer by the sun and the atmosphere. Blue: heat carried down by turbulence below the surface layer. The surface warms when red exceeds blue, and cools when blue exceeds red. From Moum et al. (2013), Fig. 3a.
:::

This matters for the climate, because surface temperature sets how much heat and moisture the ocean gives to the atmosphere. In the tropical Atlantic, it affects rainfall over West Africa and South America, the position of the Intertropical Convergence Zone and hurricane activity (Foltz et al., 2019), and even, through the atmosphere, the climate of the tropical Pacific (Rodríguez-Fonseca et al., 2009). By cooling the surface, turbulent mixing takes part in all of this.

## 3.2 Nutrients: feeding phytoplankton

Phytoplankton need light from above and nutrients from below (Cullen, 2015). In the equatorial Atlantic, turbulence is strongest in the layer between the surface and the core of the Equatorial Undercurrent, a strong eastward current below the surface (dashed line in {numref}`fig-nitrate`). Whether mixing brings nitrate up depends on where the **nitracline**, the depth below which nitrate is abundant, lies compared with this layer (Brandt et al., 2025):

- **In April**, the nitracline lies below the current core: mixing acts on nitrate-poor water, and chlorophyll stays deep.
- **In July**, the nitracline rises into the mixing layer: mixing brings nitrate up, and chlorophyll reaches the surface.

:::{figure} figures/brandt2025_fig6.png
:label: fig-nitrate
:width: 90%
The equatorial Atlantic in April (left) and July (right), in the upper 150 m, from west (W) to east (E). Dashed line: core of the Equatorial Undercurrent; turbulence is strong above it. In April, the nitracline is below this core and chlorophyll stays deep; in July, the nitracline is above it, mixing brings nitrate up, and chlorophyll reaches the surface. From Brandt et al. (2025), Fig. 6.
:::

Our profile was taken in April: its deep chlorophyll maximum, around 45 dbar, is the April case.

## 3.3 Oxygen: ventilating the oxygen minimum zone

In the eastern tropical North Atlantic, between about 350 and 550 m, lies water poor in oxygen: an **oxygen minimum zone**. There, respiration consumes oxygen, and several processes bring it back. {numref}`fig-oxygen` shows this budget: vertical mixing (black) is one of the supplies, of the same order as the others (Brandt et al., 2015).

:::{figure} figures/levy2022_fig2.png
:label: fig-oxygen
:width: 70%
Oxygen budget of the oxygen minimum zone of the eastern tropical North Atlantic, as a function of depth. Green: consumption by respiration. Black: supply by vertical (turbulent) mixing. Purple: supply by horizontal mixing. Yellow: supply by currents. Grey band: the oxygen minimum zone. From Lévy et al. (2022), Fig. 2, adapted from Brandt et al. (2015).
:::

On our profile, the oxycline below 40 dbar shows the same contrast at a smaller scale: oxygen-rich water above, oxygen-poor water below, and turbulence in between.

## 3.4 Back to the special depth

In part 1, we asked whether the coincidence of the turbulence peak with the oxygen and chlorophyll maxima was a chance. In these three examples, mixing matters because it acts where heat, nutrients and oxygen change strongly with depth. On our profile, this is the layer around 40–45 dbar. How much turbulence moves there is what we compute in part 5, after learning how $\varepsilon$ is measured (part 4).

:::{tip} Take-home
- **Turbulent mixing connects the surface to the ocean interior**: it cools the surface, brings nutrients up to phytoplankton, and brings oxygen down.
- **These effects reach the climate and marine life**: rainfall, the supply of nutrients, the oxygen of the deep ocean.
:::
